## Layer 1: The Stage — Oakmont, Massachusetts

### 1. Genesis & Bloodlines

Oakmont was incorporated in 1923, between the wars, as a standard Essex County municipal charter. It is not a private town, a company town, or an experiment. It has a mayor, a town council, a police department, and a public works department that complains about the same potholes every spring. What makes it unusual is not its governance but its density of old capital landing in a single watershed.

**The Four Founding Families**

Three British, one American. They arrived in the interwar window with European capital flight and American industrial profit, purchasing the lakefront territory and subdividing it through standard Massachusetts land trusts.

- **Beaufort**: British. Beaufort Global Capital. A financial empire with influence across international banking and investment. They manage money that predates the modern Federal Reserve architecture.
- **Montclair**: British. Montclair Asset Group. Generational wealth so old it barely maintains a public brand. They hold conservation easements, museum wings, and the quiet historical power of lenders who never borrow.
- **Carter**: American. Carter Aerospace & Defense. The loudest of the founders. Government contracts, patriotic philanthropy, and a family habit of naming public buildings after themselves.
- **Davenport**: British. A real estate empire shaping high-value properties across the UK and USA. In Oakmont, they literally own the ground beneath the town. Many residents—wealthy and working-class alike—pay ground rent or hold title subject to Davenport trust conditions without knowing it.

**The Fifth Proprietor**

The original 1923 town charter bears five signatures. A courthouse basement flood in 1947 destroyed the ledger containing the fifth family’s name and lineage. Historians know only that they contributed a two-hundred-acre tract along the northwest lakefront. At the Oakmont Historical Society’s annual Founders’ Dinner, a fifth chair sat draped in plain black cloth for seventy-five years, reserved in case an authenticated heir presented claim. That reservation expired quietly in 2022. The chair was removed to the Historical Society storage basement. The associated land had already reverted to public trust in 1952 after tax delinquency; today it forms the public beachhead of Grand Lakes District.

If a reader does not claim descent from the fifth line, the vacancy remains a local ghost story—“the Lost Quarter”—and the empty chair is simply a forgotten artifact in a basement. If a reader does claim it, the chair comes out, and the Historical Society’s bylaws trigger a review of the 1952 transfer.

**The Emerging Families**

They arrived between the 1980s and the 2010s. They do not hold founding mythos, but they hold current leverage.

- **Laurent**: French-rooted luxury and cultural empire. Fashion houses, art acquisitions, museum boards, and taste-making influence. They entered Oakmont society by underwriting the right galas.
- **Conway**: Political dynasty. Senators, governors, and federal representatives across multiple generations. The only family among the elite that still needs voters, polling data, and precinct captains.
- **Sterling**: Global media, news syndicates, press, and broadcasting networks. They shape what the country knows about Oakmont before the town knows it itself.
- **Knox**: Agricultural and food commodity giant. Co-founders (with the Parkers) of Parker & Knox Global Holdings (P&K), a digital commerce and infrastructure system rivaling Amazon in regional scope.
- **Parker**: Majority stakeholders of P&K. Expertise in emerging technologies and engineering. They built the logistics spine that delivers goods to Oakmont doorsteps and designed the smart-grid pilots in New Eastwood.
- **Hearst**: Global shipping, mining, and natural resources. A private oil company behind a wall of philanthropic foundations. Extractive wealth dressed in conservation grants.

### 2. The Nine Districts

Oakmont is segmented by topography, vintage, and invisible property lines. There are no checkpoints, no private security jurisdiction, and no GPS dead zones—only school districts, zoning variances, and parking enforcement that map cleanly onto class.

| District | Character | Anchor Residents / Landmarks |
|----------|-----------|------------------------------|
| **Grand Lakes District** | Recreational zone. Public beaches, private boathouses, summer marinas, resort cottages, and the municipal sailing club. In July, the population doubles with seasonal residents. The smell of lake weed, diesel, and charcoal grills hangs over the docks. | The "Lost Quarter" public trust land. The OUL summer showcase pitch. |
| **New Eastwood** | Modern luxury residential. Heated driveways, glass curtain walls, smart-home architecture, and underground parking. The streets are wider; the trees are younger. | Parkers and Laurents. |
| **Greater Eastwood** | Traditional elite estates. Wrought iron, gravel drives, brick and limestone facades set back behind hedgerows. The silence is older here. | Carters, Conways, and Hearsts. |
| **Riverside** | Affluent residential. Renovated Victorians, riverside condos, and the last of the old boatyard families who sold out at the right time. The commuter rail station sits here. | Sterlings and Knoxs. |
| **Kingsridge** | High-value academic and commercial heart. Charlesworth University’s main campus, the downtown hotel strip, the enclosed shopping arcade, and the municipal parking structures that fund the town budget. ### 3. The Fourth Season

Oakmont recognizes four distinct annual phases, but autumn is locally renamed for its meteorological reality.

- **Spring:** Thaw and mud. Lake Ashford ice breaks up in late March. The seasonal estates of Grand Lakes are opened by caretakers before the families return from winter homes. Charlesworth University’s spring term begins. It is a season of lease renegotiation, landscaping crews, and the first OUL friendlies.
- **Summer:** The lake owns the town. Sailing regattas, public beach overcrowding, and the temporary population of summer residents who rent the New Eastwood condos. Kingsridge becomes a commercial carnival. Riverside smells like grilled fish and outboard motor exhaust. Local businesses inflate prices without shame.
- **Rain:** The dominant season. A low gray sky from mid-September through November. Fog that sits on Lake Ashford until noon. Decaying leaves pasted to cobblestone by constant drizzle. This is the OUL regular season. Wool overcoats, whiskey flasks at pitchside, tires slipping on the old roads. The town turns inward. Relationships formed in summer either end or deepen.
- **Winter:** The lake freezes by late December. The freight spur keeps Southbrook moving, but the town’s social life contracts to indoor spaces. Galas at Greater Eastwood estates. Study marathons in Education District libraries. The commuter rail runs reduced service. Fires in marble fireplaces. Cabin fever.

### 4. The Town Religion: Soccer & The OUL

American football exists on television. In Oakmont, **soccer is the public obsession**.

The **Oakmont University League (OUL)** is an eight-team amateur conference representing the eight universities and colleges within the town’s orbit. The capital flowing through it—alumni donor wars, local betting pools, apparel licensing, and recruitment pipelines—behaves like a professional operation with amateur tax status.

On match Saturdays, the town shuts down procedurally: police reroute traffic, pubs open at 8:00 AM, and a municipal ordinance permits sidewalk open-container within a quarter-mile of any OUL ground.

**The Eight Clubs:**

- **Charlesworth Kings:** Old money pedigree, historic success, recent slump. Their ground in Kingsridge is a stone bowl that seats eight thousand. They are the default aristocrats of the league.
- **Westbridge Titans:** Methodical, data-driven, rooted in Old Oakmont’s technical institute. Their supporters bring analytics to the pub.
- **Blackridge United:** Southbrook’s team. Grit, compact defending, and a working-class supporter base that treats matches as territorial assertions.
- **Eastwood Lions:** The Education District’s art-and-humanities college. Flamboyant, chaotic, beautiful to watch, unreliable in results.
- **Harrisfield Falcons:** A design and performance school in the Education District. Theatrical kits, theatrical fans, and a pitch that hosts more cultural events than sports.
- **Northington Vipers:** The business-school outfit in the Education District. They recruit aggressively, spend creatively, and are under investigation by the league for third-party sponsorship every other year.
- **Southbrook Hurricanes:** Oakmont Community College’s team. No budget, a municipal pitch that floods during Rain season, and a supporter base that treats relegation battles like last stands.
- **Redford Legends:** The party school from the Education District. Perpetually underachieving, perpetually talented, perpetually involved in disciplinary hearings.

Every resident claims a team. It is the fastest way to locate someone on the town’s social grid.

### 5. Daily Texture & Grounded Rules

Oakmont operates like a standard Massachusetts municipality with extremely wealthy residents. The class divide is visible in infrastructure, not in conspiracy.

- **Governance:** An elected mayor and seven-person town council. Zoning meetings are standing-room-only because multi-million dollar views depend on them. The police department is a standard municipal force; the chief is appointed by the council.
- **Transit:** Interstate access is via a single state highway that floods during heavy Rain and ices over in Winter. The commuter rail to Boston runs on a reduced Sunday schedule on major OUL match days to manage platform crowding.
- **Property:** Davenport ground trusts still appear in title searches. New Eastwood and Northwood Height have active homeowners’ associations with legitimate legal authority. Fines for unapproved paint colors are real and enforceable.
- **Commerce:** The Oakmont Public Market in Greater Eastwood operates under 1923 charter rules: individual butchers, fishmongers, florists. Chain retail is concentrated in Kingsridge. Delivery culture is stratified by district; same-day P&K delivery is universal, but leaving cardboard boxes on a Greater Eastwood porch is considered a neighborly offense.
- **Alcohol:** Last call is 1:00 AM by town ordinance. The real drinking happens in private residences, Grand Lakes boathouses, and the Iron Lantern Pub in Southbrook, where the owner does not check IDs if you can name the starting eleven of any OUL side.
- **Technology:** Municipal fiber was installed via a Parker infrastructure grant. Usage is standard. The Founding Families still maintain unlisted landlines and paper filing systems out of habit, not paranoia.
