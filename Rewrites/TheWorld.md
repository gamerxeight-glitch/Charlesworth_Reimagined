## Oakmont

*Massachusetts, United States*

### The Ground

Oakmont lies inland in Massachusetts, well out of reach of the coast and content to be. The town sits on high, wooded ground between three lakes — the largest giving the Grand Lakes district its name — and the land around it is granite, pine, and quiet money.

Residences sit far back from private roads. Hotels face the water. Nothing is crowded. Nothing is accidental. Oakmont was planned, and it has been maintained ever since.

### The Founding

The town was established after the Second World War by five families who arrived together and stayed. They were American and European by origin, wealthy before they came, and more interested in permanence than display. They laid the roads, raised the institutions, and built the estates first — and only afterward let a town grow up around them.

Four of those five names still carry Oakmont: **Beaufort**, **Carter**, **Montclair**, and **Davenport**. The fifth is a matter this account leaves closed.

In the decades that followed, other households rose to join the founders. **Conway**, through politics. **Laurent**, through culture and craft. **Sterling**, through land and building. They arrived during the town's second expansion and were eventually recognized alongside the original five — a second founding, in all but ceremony. One more name belongs to this tier and is likewise left unspoken here.

More recently a third class of family has begun to settle: **Parker** and **Knox**, whose holdings in commerce and infrastructure made them unavoidable long before they were welcomed, and the household of a new arrival to the university, still unrecorded. These are the emerging names — established enough to matter, not yet deep enough to be taken for granted.

The tiers of the family network describe influence, not money. Old money is not better than new money in Oakmont. It is simply older — and the town has a long memory.

### The Seasons

Oakmont takes its year in four turns, and the town changes shape with each one.

**Spring** arrives wet and sudden. Snowmelt comes down off the wooded hills, the lake ice breaks in the shallows, and the lawns go green before the trees do. Lawn crews are out early on the private estates. Allergies and optimism, in equal measure. The town shakes off its winter posture and remembers how to gather.

**Summer** slows it down. The lake houses fill, the resorts along Grand Lakes do their briskest trade of the year, and the roads out of Oakmont stay busy from Friday to Sunday. Long light past nine in the evening. Cut grass and hot stone. Even the dynasties loosen their calendar a little — though not much.

**Rain** is Oakmont's name for autumn, and it earns the name. The rains settle in and stay — not storms, but a grey, persistent drizzle that softens the town for weeks at a stretch. Undergrounded leaves turn the streets to copper. Fog sits on the lake in the mornings. Everything moves indoors — meetings, dinners, rivalries, romance. If the town has a season of its own, it is this one.

**Winter** is clean and cutting. Frost on granite. Snow off the hills. The lakes freezing over around the edges first. Long blue evenings lit by windows. Residences close their ranks. Old money, in Oakmont, looks most like itself in winter.

### The Look of the Town

Oakmont's architecture tells its story whether anyone intended it to.

**Old Oakmont** is stone and slate. The administrative quarter is low and civic, with broad brick footpaths and brass fixtures gone green up. Government offices, the older storefronts, the original town hall clock tower. Rossi's Italian Kitchen has held the same corner since before the second expansion.

**Greater Eastwood** and **Northwood Heights** are estate country: set-back driveways, iron gates, long lawns, mature oaks left standing on purpose. The older estates use stone and timber and do not advertise what they cost. The newer ones in Northwood lean classical European — columned, symmetrical, curated to look as though they have always been there.

**New Eastwood** is the outlier. Glass. Steel. Slender residential towers shot up in a single generation, catching the lake light in the late afternoon like a second, shakier skyline. Locals have opinions about New Eastwood. They rarely say them out loud.

**Riverside** sits between the two poles — modern but restrained, all clean lines, dark wood, and low lighting. Architecture designed to be lived in, not looked at.

**Downtown** is the one part of Oakmont that belongs to everyone. Iron fronts and awnings along Maple Row. Bloom in the window boxes come spring. Strings of small lights over the square in winter. The kind of main street that was planned to look charming and now simply is.

**Southbrook** is honest about what it is: brick, concrete, block glass, roll-up doors. Auto shops, yards, warehouses. The town pretends Southbrook is only industrial. Southbrook is where a great deal of what Oakmont actually needs gets built and mended.

**The lakefront at Grand Lakes** speaks its own grammar: cedar-shingled boathouses, floating docks, resorts with shallow stone terraces facing the water. In summer, sailboats and white light on the surface. In winter, the bare docks, grey and quietly beautiful.

### The Life of the Town

Mornings in Oakmont smell of coffee and cut grass. Downtown fills first — engineers and lawyers crossing from Maple Row toward the civic quarter, joggers along the lakeshore path, shop owners wiping down their storefronts with the particular concentration of people who have done it for twenty years. Maple & Bean Café opens at six and is full by seven, and by eight the queue has stopped being a queue and become a conversation.

By mid-morning the estates are working: gardeners on the lawns, deliveries on the long driveways, a steady line of black cars moving between the residential districts and Kingsridge. The town runs on appointments and punctuality, and the people in it maintain full calendars even on days that seem to call for nothing at all.

Afternoons loosen the reins. Riverside fills with walkers. The shops on Maple Row do their real business after three. The Iron Lantern Pub starts pouring at four and by six looks like it has been full for hours — which, in fairness, it has. Along Grand Lakes the light turns long and gold, and the resorts begin setting their terraces for dinner.

Evenings are quiet, but never empty. Dinner parties behind closed doors in Greater Eastwood. Light on the water. Someone returning late down a private drive with headlamps sweeping the trees. Oakmont is not a small town that goes dark; it is a town that goes indoors and continues.

What keeps it alive is not any single event, but small constant things. Bells from the Old Oakmont clock tower on the hour. Ice cream on the pier in summer, hot cider on the same pier in October. Bicycles on the lake path in spring, left unlocked and found again. A florist on Maple Row who knows every anniversary in the dynasties and will never say so. Local dogs who know exactly which gates to wait at.

### The Table

Food in Oakmont is an argument conducted in courses. The dynasties have houses and kitchens of their own, but the town itself speaks in three names.

**Rossi's Italian Kitchen** — Old Oakmont, holding its corner forever. Little has changed: red-checked linen, a house red better than it has any need to be, bread that arrives before anyone has asked for it, and a plate of *cacio e pepe* on the menu under an older name the family will translate for you if they like you. Fortuna Rossi runs the kitchen now, the third Rossi to do so; her father, by her own account, "only ever made one dish wrong and we don't discuss it."

**Maple & Bean** — Downtown, on the corner of Maple Row with the good light. Coffee and pastries with a quiet cult following among students and professionals alike. The Maple & Bean morning bun is the informal currency of Oakmont's downtime: everyone has opinions, everyone sends one to someone, no one discusses it openly.

**The Iron Lantern** — Downtown, the pub that outlasts every restaurant opening nearby. Built in an old ironworks, lit by actual iron lanterns, pouring local brew and running a short menu of things done exactly right. A steak sandwich built its reputation, a winter chowder that appears in November and disappears in March, and a Sunday board that has changed at least one opinion about at least one family.

The lakefront at Grand Lakes keeps its own summer register — grilled fish, corn, cold white wine on the water, ice cream at the pier shack that opens the week the lake ice breaks. In Rain season, Oakmont eats indoors and heavier; Rossi's runs a wild mushroom risotto that appears only in October. The town's rhythm is edible.

Beneath it all runs P&K — the delivery system bringing food, groceries, and everything else to whichever door you specify. In Oakmont, P&K is infrastructure, not a service. Even the dynasties rely on it.
